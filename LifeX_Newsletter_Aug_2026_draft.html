Greetings from LifeX

A strange thing is happening in American science. Congress mostly protected the money. The machinery that turns the money into experiments has become less predictable. At the same time, Washington is assembling a different machine: federal datasets, supercomputers, foundation models, and robotic laboratories pointed at national scientific missions.

That tension is interesting to us at LifeX. Old pipelines are becoming less reliable just as AI makes entirely new ones possible. Below: what the Trump administration's science agenda may change, why NoTraffic could own an unusual world-model asset, fifteen businesses hiding inside a rare dataset, and the fresh portfolio and market signals moving through the LifeX chat.

What we're thinking: one budget, two operating systems

The administration proposed cuts of roughly 40% at NIH and more than 50% at NSF. Congress rejected most of that: NIH landed around $48.7 billion, slightly above the prior year, while NSF absorbed a much smaller reduction.

But appropriations and actual grantmaking are not the same thing. Awards have moved unevenly, review and administrative capacity have been disrupted, and researchers are making career decisions before the system catches up.

$48.7B
NIH funding survived Congress. Grantmaking still slowed.
$5B+
New federal commitments to the Genesis Mission for AI-driven science.

Then came the other half of the story. The White House expanded the Genesis Mission: more than fifteen agencies contributing research funding, scientific datasets, and facilities to a national AI-for-science effort. The accompanying science report calls for domain-specific foundation models, AI-enabled verification, autonomous laboratories, and AI-native scientific institutions.

This is not simply “science funding up” or “science funding down.” It is a change in the shape of science. Less predictable investigator-led grantmaking on one side; more mission-directed data, compute, and automation on the other.

Two consequences we are underwriting:

Talent is unusually available. Translational scientists whose grants or institutions have stalled are moving toward startups. That is a hiring market for founders and a sourcing market for us.

Private data and execution capability become more valuable. If the public discovery pipeline gets lumpier, a company that already owns a hard-to-recreate cohort, instrument network, or experimental loop gains strategic value.

Regulatory variance

The FDA's outside compounding panel recently recommended allowing BPC-157, KPV, and several other peptides despite objections from agency scientists. The vote is advisory, not an approval—but it is another sign that “what the system will permit” is becoming a less stable input to a health-company plan.

We are not making a political point. We are making an allocation point: when institutions change, the location of durable scientific value changes with them.

Portfolio Highlight: NoTraffic and the world model at the intersection

Frontier AI labs want world models: systems that understand not only words and pictures, but how physical space behaves over time. Most spatial training data comes from satellites, aerial imagery, or vehicles passing through a scene.

NoTraffic sits at the intersection. Literally. Its sensors and edge devices are installed in traffic infrastructure, looking outward from a fixed position, continuously, in every condition, for years.

A passing car sees an intersection for four seconds. Infrastructure sees the negotiation of that space for a decade.

Pedestrians, cyclists, delivery vans, emergency vehicles, near misses, weather, construction—the whole multi-agent choreography of a city, with temporal depth that neither a satellite nor a vehicle fleet can reproduce.

NoTraffic raised a $90 million Series C led by PSG Equity, with M&G, Grove, LifeX, and others. Deployment was expected to reach roughly one in ten traffic agencies across the United States and Canada by midyear. Congestion relief and safer streets are already a serious business.

But underneath that business, the company is accumulating something else: a persistent corpus of physical-world behavior. The traffic system may also be a spatial-intelligence system. We think the second asset is not yet fully priced.

Fifteen businesses hiding inside a rare dataset

This question comes up constantly in AI × Bio: you have a genuinely rare dataset—how do you turn it into revenue without giving away the thing that makes it valuable?

The default answer is a “bio-bucks” partnership: one large counterparty, a modest upfront, and a long tail of milestones that may never arrive. We mapped a more durable stack: five businesses at each of three layers.

Data / sell controlled access
1. Annual subscription
2. Per-cohort license
3. Usage-based clean room
4. Benchmark dataset
5. Synthetic derivative license
Comparable patterns: Tempus, Flatiron, Komodo, nference, Integral, ChartR
Model / sell the answer
6. Inference API
7. Target or biomarker reports
8. Trial-enrichment score
9. Validation service
10. Outcome-priced decision support
Comparable patterns: Tessel, Modella, Owkin, HealX
Distribution / own the workflow
11. Workflow software
12. Sponsored professional access
13. Marketplace take rate
14. Exclusive asset spinout
15. Network-powered discovery platform
Comparable patterns: OpenEvidence, Science Bridge

The higher the layer, the better it protects the underlying asset—and the more recurring the business can become.

23andMe is the cautionary case. It built around one deep pharma relationship, then watched the partnership narrow into a much smaller data license. Without enough diversified recurring revenue, the company eventually entered Chapter 11 and sold its assets.

The lesson is not “do not partner.” It is: build the recurring, diversified, IP-protected lines before the deep partnership becomes the only thing holding the company up.

Portfolio adjacency: where the market is validating the thesis

Silicon photonics moved from technical necessity to a market headline.
Jensen Huang on copper versus optics
Huang's formulation was unusually crisp: use copper wherever you can and optics wherever you must. Wall Street heard the second half. For LifeX portfolio company Xscape Photonics, this is another sign that optical interconnects are becoming required infrastructure for scaling AI, not a speculative edge case.

Regulated life-sciences workflows are becoming an AI battleground.
Collate raised a $95 million Series A
The round validates a category we keep seeing around portfolio companies such as Bluenote and Peer AI: the valuable product is not a clever first draft. It is trusted software that can execute inside regulated, auditable workflows. Overheard at LifeX: “Peer AI and Bluenote likely to follow with big rounds soon.”

Immune data is becoming infrastructure.
IMU Biosciences completed a $53 million Series A
IMU says a blood sample can yield more than 100 million immune data points. The round is interesting less as another “AI biotech” financing than as evidence for the rare-data thesis above: longitudinal, high-resolution biological measurement can become a platform before it becomes a drug.

The operating room is becoming a general-purpose robotics environment.
Humanoid robots completed two teleoperated surgeries in a preclinical study
The procedures were performed in large non-primate mammals, not people, and the robots required recalibration. Still, this points toward a stack where general-purpose embodiment meets surgical perception—the environment LifeX portfolio company Hypervision Surgical is helping make legible. Overheard at LifeX: “The robots are now doing surgeries!”

Consumer health keeps finding substantial wedges inside familiar measurements.
Dexcom's Stelo reportedly reached $150 million in revenue in roughly eighteen months
A direct-to-consumer continuous glucose monitor becoming material to a public company's revenue this quickly is a distribution signal. Sensors that were once prescribed for disease management are becoming everyday consumer-health interfaces.

Markets & Signals

Male contraception finally attracts late-stage capital.
Contraline raised $92.5 million
An eleven-year path to a large Series B is not overnight success. It is a reminder that neglected health categories can look niche until product risk falls and direct-to-consumer distribution becomes plausible. Overheard at LifeX: “Maybe we should have invested.”

Preventive cardiology is getting a consumer front door.
Hale is building a doctor-led preventive heart scan
The important product question is whether a scan can become a recurring care pathway rather than a premium one-time image. We are watching how this develops alongside the broader Function Health, Biograph, and consumer-diagnostics category.

De-extinction may be a platform business wearing a spectacular costume.
Colossal was reportedly discussing a $20–30 billion valuation
The serious assets are conservation tools, computational biology, artificial-womb technology, and spinouts. The unserious—but perhaps not wrong—LifeX interpretation: “There will be a lot of theme parks, I guess.”

AI is compressing the cost and time of customer research.
A signal from the emerging AI-native research category
Studies that once cost seven figures and took six months are becoming software workflows. That is bad news for agencies selling process and good news for anyone who owns differentiated respondents, distribution, or proprietary data. Overheard at LifeX: “It's only one year old!”
